**Pake packaging finally comes to the desktop on every system**
<https://pake.run/>

**Notes from Kuala Lumpur, the things that were different from what I expected**

<video poster="https://weekly.tw93.fun/assets/posters/a9eb0f61f219.v2.webp" width="800px" preload="metadata" controls><source src="https://cdn.tw93.fun/notes/13_2124366670596857856.mp4" type="video/mp4"></video>

Every time I come back from a trip abroad, I want to write something to chat with you about where what I actually saw differed from what I had imagined. It's really interesting, and maybe that's the point of traveling. While it's still fresh, let me tell you about the things in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, that were different from what I expected.

1. Kuala Lumpur is quite a bit more developed than I expected. It feels a little like Guangzhou, and in the core areas it even feels like Shanghai, but daily life is a lot like Chongqing: the locals take their time, and the terrain is a lot like Chongqing too, so you often walk in from the street and find yourself on the third floor of a building.

2. There are lots of gas cars and very few EVs. A local friend told me fuel in Malaysia costs only about 1/3 of what it does in China, so nearly everything on the road burns gas because it's so cheap. People who drive EVs like a Tesla are usually the new tech rich. I didn't see a single Tesla those few days, and even a BYD EV counts as a pretty nice car there.

3. With plenty of water and power, quite a few global tech companies build data centers around here, and AWS, Microsoft and Google all have very large ones. As for internet and tech companies, Grab, the ride-hailing app you usually use in Southeast Asia, started here, and Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Shopee, Lazada and Alipay all have offices here, basically in very upscale spots.

4. Speaking of Grab, fuel is cheap, but labor here seems a fair bit more expensive than in China. Rides cost about the same as in Shanghai, though the cars may be a bit worse. Grab there is a bit like Didi and Meituan food delivery rolled into one, and people even suggest booking Grab inside Alipay because it matches you with a Chinese-speaking driver. Lots of locals ride motorcycles too. I often saw young guys flying along the highway on motorbikes, and plenty of people ride even in heavy rain, probably because it's simply how they get around.

5. The weather in Kuala Lumpur is interesting. Before I left, the forecast said heat and heavy rain, but the downpours those days stopped very quickly, even though the rain was really heavy. After the rain it's very comfortable and cool, and when the sun is out it's very hot, a bit like summer in Hangzhou, while indoor places like malls crank the AC way up. I learned Malaysia has just two seasons, dry and rainy, and this happened to be the rainy season.

6. There are a lot more foreigners in Kuala Lumpur than I thought: Malays, ethnic Chinese, Indians, Europeans and Americans, plus plenty of Japanese and Koreans. Malay looks a lot like English, but you'll still need a translation app for the local language. Luckily many shops have Chinese staff, so speaking Chinese is no problem at all, and if your English is good, living here is no problem either.

7. As for the Malay locals, the government benefits are actually quite good. All jobs in government and public institutions, the equivalent of our civil servants, are open only to locals, so in many public departments you see a lot of local Muslim staff. Ethnic Chinese and foreigners in Kuala Lumpur mostly do business, and they tend to work a lot harder than the locals.

8. Malaysia is visa-free, which makes the trip very convenient. I was worried I'd need cash and exchanged 600 ringgit at the airport, only to find out later that I'd lost out. For a holiday I don't think you need to exchange money at all, you just lose on the rate. Alipay handles nearly everything, many restaurants don't even take cash, and Alipay and WeChat Pay are more than enough.

9. As for daily habits, life here moves very slowly and people are relaxed about time. They love sweet things, there are plenty of milk tea chains from China, plus lots of local milk tea and ice cream shops, and I found it all a bit too sweet. Cars also drive on the left with the steering wheel on the right, the opposite of China, so you look right first when crossing the road, which took me a while to get used to.

10. The local Chinese are very warm and kind. After the Li Zhi concert I booked a Grab, and the driver was an elderly Chinese man. He patiently sent me photos of where the car was, waited quite a while, and even got out to wait for me. At restaurants too, the Chinese staff were all very polite when I ordered.

11. Now some recommendations for where to stay and eat. For a place to stay, I recommend Bukit Bintang or KLCC. This time I stayed at the Marriott in Bukit Bintang. The two areas are very close, and you can walk to Pavilion next door for food and shopping. In KLCC the Petronas Twin Towers are right there, and Suria KLCC, the mall at the foot of the towers, is also huge and great to wander around.

12. The food here is pretty varied, though I found the rice not as good as in China. I recommend the noodles here, and I really recommend Mama Lek, it's delicious. The beef noodles are good too, and the Western food is fine. If you want rice, try nasi lemak with a big grilled chicken leg, it's tasty. At Thong Bowl I had noodles that were way too spicy, so if you can't handle spice, remember to order mild. Funnily enough, I ended up eating 2 kinds of Chinese meat pies here, and the beef and chicken pies in a mall basement were really good, which cracked me up. The coffee here is good too, so be sure to find a few places for a coffee, it's very pleasant.

13. For sightseeing, I recommend Merdeka Square, a rather solemn place, which I believe is where the Malaysian flag was first raised. Tourists can go up and take photos everywhere, and there are quite a few Western cafés inside. I think it's great for photos overall, like the picture below. I also recommend the KLCC Twin Towers, where there's an infinity pool you can book for a swim. And I think finding a quiet spot for a walk in Kuala Lumpur on a cool morning is really pleasant.

**How I turned my computers and phones into a really handy Agent development setup**

I suddenly really wanted to share how I turned the old computers and old phone at home, together with my main Mac, into a really handy setup for Agent work and development.

A year ago, being the neat freak I am, I'd probably have sold all these old devices on Xianyu and kept only my main Mac. But this year the software I build covers Mac, Windows, Linux and iPhone, and these devices have helped me a lot.

The devices

My main machine is a black Mac with an M5 Pro, 48 GB of memory and 1 TB of storage. It's great, I bought it this year, and my only regret is not getting the 2 TB version. Writing code, signing and notarizing, and shipping releases all happen on it, and it's the only machine I use myself.

The test machine is an M2 Pro with 32 GB of memory and 512 GB, the one I used before buying the M5. It then sat gathering dust in a cabinet for a few months. In October I was testing Mole and needed an Agent to do a lot of hands-on computer work for me. Doing that on my main machine felt like someone was fighting me for my computer, and when I finally couldn't take it anymore, I had the Agent move all the testing work to this machine.

The M2 test machine also has an iPhone SE3 plugged in. With iPhone Use and iPhone Mirroring, AI can operate it really well. It mainly handles testing for the iOS version of MiaoYan, plus all kinds of phone testing for new products. The Agent can also use WebDriverAgent to read the phone screen as individual elements and tap them by label, which works great.

And that's not all, I've used this test machine to the limit. It also runs a Windows 11 ARM virtual machine in Parallels, which is where the ARM64 version of Mole for Windows gets tested.

Then there's a Windows ThinkPad X1 with 8 GB of memory, which I got while writing Mole for Windows. I use it to test on real x64 hardware, things like screen scaling, battery, SmartScreen and Defender that you can't see in a virtual machine, and release acceptance for the Windows version also needs this real Windows machine.

To get more out of it, I also installed an Omarchy Linux virtual machine on it to test how Pake behaves on a Wayland desktop. Since it only has 8 GB, I try to have the Agent use only one system per task.

Finally, there's a NAS mini host running a Debian 12 container, x86_64 with 8 cores and 31 GB, dedicated to Linux command lines, GNU tool differences, and Pake's deb and rpm packages. BSD sed on the Mac and GNU sed on Linux differ in a lot of ways, and this machine is the safety net for all of that.

How I use them for testing

Still being a neat freak who doesn't want so much stuff on my desk, I put all these test machines in a cabinet in the spare bedroom. Everything is plugged in and well ventilated, the screens are at the lowest brightness, and they're set to never sleep or turn off the display, though every two weeks or so I go and restart all of them.

First, I use them to test all my software. I've always believed that a release should be a proper one, and what ships should be stable, so I spend a lot of time testing every release myself. Before my own testing, I have the Agent run everything strictly on real machines in all kinds of environments, and once that's clean, I test it again by hand. That way the software that reaches you meets the bar.

When the Agent tested UI on my main machine, it fought me for the mouse: whenever it moved the mouse, grabbed focus or switched windows, I couldn't use my computer, which was really awkward, and that's why I set up all the devices above. At the end of October I moved every job that needs to control the mouse to the test machine. UI tests, end-to-end tests and clicking system permission dialogs all happen there, and the main machine only builds and sends the packages over.

The Agent doesn't operate the test machine by guessing click positions from screenshots. It uses two very small command-line tools I wrote. ax goes through the macOS accessibility API, and something like ax tree <pid> prints every element in an App window, with its identifier, text and center coordinates, as text, so it can press buttons by identifier, and very accurately. input sends mouse and keyboard events through CGEvent, for the things the accessibility tree can't press, like context menus, typing into a search box, or pressing Esc to close a menu. In effect, what's on the test machine gets turned into text context that's easier for the Agent to understand. It only takes a screenshot when nothing else works, and the rest of the time this approach is much faster.

How I use them to save money

Over the last 3 months my GitHub Actions CI costs went way over, around $70 or more a month, which started to hurt a bit. I once shared how to make CI on GitHub Actions faster and cheaper, but that didn't go far enough, because for private repos, GitHub-hosted macOS runners are billed at ten times the minutes, and native builds and tests burn money fast.

So in early November I put a self-hosted runner on the test machine for each of my private repos. For example, one make verify run of Mole Mac takes over 20 minutes, Pake's Mac tests aren't fast either, and there are MiaoYan's iOS builds and so on. All of it moved to the local test machine. So far this M2 test machine has picked up nearly 180 jobs, and what used to cost money is now handled locally.

To save even more, I also added a check: a tiny job that takes only a few seconds runs on Ubuntu first and asks GitHub whether the current commit on the main branch already has a finished full test run. If it does, it stops right there, and only if not does it hand the work to the test machine.

There's one small catch: CI and the Agent are actually sharing one screen, so they have to take turns. I made a "busy" marker file. Every time the Agent drives the test machine through a script, it refreshes this file and records which project is using it. When a CI job starts, for example, it first grabs a "CI busy" marker, created with noclobber, so only one side can get it at a time, and there are retries and timeouts as well, so the test machine can keep working 24 hours a day.

Agents also fight each other over the same screen. Luckily, conversations can now message each other. Before using the screen, an Agent checks whether anyone is on it, asks the other one roughly when it'll be done, and then waits patiently, only checking again when the time is nearly up. There are lots of little clever touches like these, which is really fun, so what's holding back my testing now is probably that I still don't have enough machines, haha, though what I have is enough.

What else is good about this?

Back to what I posted yesterday: on this trip I didn't even bring my main machine, just a family member's very light Air, and it was completely enough. I even feel it was far more convenient than bringing my main machine. If I'd brought it, it would have been off on the road and on the plane, unable to do anything, and switching countries can also make Claude a bit unstable. Now these machines are on 24 hours a day, working and running all the time, and the bottleneck isn't the machines anymore, it's my own attention.

Still, I feel that in the end, the point of all this should be to make life easier and work more efficient, leaving more time for what I want to do, rather than throwing myself into it full time, which would be putting the cart before the horse. I really recommend everyone work more efficiently and free up time for more things away from the computer, that's what makes life interesting.

Good health > a happy life > happy coding. They all matter a lot, but there should still be priorities, so that you have the energy to chase interesting things beyond code.
